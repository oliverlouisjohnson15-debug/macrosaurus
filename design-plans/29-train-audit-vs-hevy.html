Train Module Teardown

Every screen in Train, what it is for, and where two screens are doing one screen's job — measured against Hevy, which solves the same problem with a quarter of the surface area.

Method

Counted, not eyeballed

Every number here comes from mounting the real components in jsdom through the project's own test harness and enumerating what they draw — the same technique train-buttons.test.js uses to find dead controls. A "control" is a leaf button, input or select: a card that merely contains buttons is not counted, so these are things a thumb can actually land on.

The Hevy comparison is drawn from its public help centre and feature pages, linked at each claim. Where Hevy simply has no equivalent, that is stated rather than counted as a win for either side.

The shape of the problem

Train is sixteen destinations

16routes in the Train router
149controls across 14 of them
4screens answering "how am I doing"
5doors to getting a plan

Hevy runs the entire app — social feed, logging, history, statistics, exercise library — on four tabs. Training lives in two of them: Workout holds routines and starts sessions, Profile holds history, statistics and the exercise list (how Hevy organises logged workouts, the exercise library).

Our Train tab alone is sixteen. Some of that is real: we generate and audit programmes, and Hevy does not. But four of the sixteen answer the same question, and five are ways of arriving at the same object.

Part one

The user guide

What the module actually does, in the order you meet it. This is the sequence a new block travels: get a plan, run it, tune it.

Getting a plan

Macrosaurus programmes

TrainHome → a programme card → BlockBuilder

Two written four-week programmes, taken off the shelf whole. The fastest route to training on a Monday morning: no questions, the movements the programme leaves open stay open. Tapping one opens the builder on it, where you save or start it.

Build a block

TrainHome → "Build a … block" → BlockWizard → BlockBuilder

The generator. Asks how many days, what shape, min-max or volume, then writes the block against your gym's equipment and your volume bands. Can also read a plan you already follow — from a file, a link, pasted text, or screenshots — and adapt it to your day count.

Browse blocks

TrainHome → "Browse blocks" → BlockLibrary

Blocks other people have shared, filterable by days per week and split. Adopting one drops you in the builder with it as a draft.

Your blocks

TrainHome → "Blocks" → BlockList

Everything saved, running or archived. From here a block can be opened for editing, started, deleted, or inspected two ways: What it covers (CoverageScreen) and its review.

Running it

The week

TrainHome → the block card

The week you are in: a meter, a sentence, and one chip per session carrying its day and its state. Any chip starts that session today — the gold button is a recommendation, not the only offer. The week switcher above reads any week of the block without entering the editor.

The day, before you start it

a day chip → SessionPreview

Every movement, its sets, reps, tempo and effort target. Editable here: sets and reps, order, add, remove, and swap — with swaps asking whether they are for today or for the whole block. Moving a session to another weekday also lives here.

The session

"Start …" → SessionPlayer

One movement open at a time, the rest folded into Up next. Tick a set and the rest timer starts. The header carries the back link, your buddy, the session name, More, and the spine — one cell per movement, filling as you work through it.

Each movement's own menu offers eight actions: replace it, sets and reps, log warm-up sets, superset with the next, break the superset, move earlier, move later, remove.

More holds the whole-session actions: add a movement, session notes, rest timer on or off, finish, and step out for now — which keeps the session open so you can look at anything else and come back to it.

Looking back, and tuning

History, Stats, Coverage, Review

TrainHome → History / Stats · BlockList → a block → covers / review

Four screens. History is your best per lift plus past sessions. Stats is one overall strength number against bodyweight. Coverage is hard sets per muscle per week, planned or last-7-days. Review is how a finished block actually went against what it promised. See finding 1 — these overlap.

Training settings

TrainHome → gear icon → TrainSettings

Weight units. Experience level, which sets the volume bands your coverage is judged against. The gyms you train at, each with its equipment. Three in-session toggles including the rest timer. The 17-muscle volume band table. And a link to How your plan is built — nineteen rows of training doctrine.

Part two

Where two screens do one job

Ranked by how much simplification is available, not by how broken anything is. Nothing here is a bug; all of it is surface area.

Finding 1

Four screens answer "how am I doing"

Stats, History, Coverage and Review are four destinations for one question. Two of them render the same metric in the same format: sets per muscle against a band.

  • Coverage draws Lats 8 / 4-8, Glutes 8 / 4-8
  • Review draws Chest 0 / 4-8, Front delts 0 / 2-6

Same table, different date window — Coverage shows planned or last-7-days, Review shows the block's actual average. And StatSheet is a whole route for a single number: it has exactly one control, the back link.

Hevy puts all of this in one place: Profile holds the dashboard, statistics and the workout history list together (gym performance tracking).

Do: fold Stats into History as a header band — it is one number and needs no route. Make Coverage a date-window switch on one screen rather than two screens with the same table. Reach both from the same place; today two are on home and two are buried behind Blocks → a block, which is why they feel undiscoverable.
Finding 2

Five doors, one object

Programme cards, Library, Wizard, Draft and Builder are five entrances that all end in the same place — a block in BlockBuilder. Two of them appear twice: Browse blocks and Build a block render both on the empty-state grid and in the block card's footer.

Hevy has two: New Routine and Explore (building a program).

Do: one "New block" entry that opens a chooser — write me one / bring my own / take one off the shelf. The Draft card stays, because an unfinished import is a promise you made yourself and deserves to be visible. This is presentation, not deletion: every generator path survives, behind one door instead of five.
Finding 3

Settings mixes preferences with algorithm tuning

Hevy's workout settings are fourteen mechanical preferences — timer volume, keep awake, plate calculator, plate and dumbbell rounding, warm-up calculator, units, RPE tracking, PR notifications, calendar start day, smart superset scrolling (the full list). Every one changes how logging feels.

Ours mixes those with experience level and a 17-muscle volume band table. Those are not preferences — they configure the model that writes your plans, and they sit at the same visual weight as kg/lb.

Do: split the card. Preferences (units, timers, gyms) stay. Experience and the bands become "How your plans get written", grouped with the doctrine screen they explain — which is where somebody looking to change them would actually go.
Finding 4

HowItWorks is a manual, not a screen

Nineteen accordion rows of training doctrine — "Reps before weight", "Stalling is a movement problem, not a volume problem", "Half a set for helping" — behind Settings, at the end of the module. It is the second-most-controlled screen in Train and the least likely to be found.

Hevy has no equivalent because it has no opinion to explain; the app doesn't write your plan. Ours does, so the doctrine has to live somewhere.

Do: keep every word, but surface the rows where they apply — the RIR explanation next to an RIR target, the stalling rule next to a stalled lift. What survives as a screen is the index. This is the cheapest big win in the module: the content is already written and currently nobody reads it.
Part three

What not to simplify

An audit that only subtracts would take the product with it. Three things Train does that Hevy has no equivalent for, and that the surface-area count should not be used against:

  • Generation. Hevy's routines are templates you write. Ours are written for you, against your gym, your days and your recovery. The wizard is the product.
  • Coverage. Nothing in Hevy tells you a muscle is under its band. The screen should be consolidated, not removed.
  • Autoregulation. The readiness prompt at the top of a session — run it as written, trim it, ease off — reads your sleep. Hevy cannot; it has no such data.

The SessionPlayer's 23 controls also stay. It is the busiest screen in Train and that is correct: most of the count is per-set weight and rep inputs, which is the job. Hevy's active workout is comparably dense for the same reason.
